What slow breathing does for your body

Lymph flow is one benefit of this practice. Here are the others, with a label showing how strong the evidence is for each.

Well establishedGood trialsEarly evidence
Well established

Settles your nervous system

Breathing slowly, around 6 breaths a minute, turns up the vagus nerve. That's the body's "rest and digest" side. Your heart slows a little on every out-breath, which is why a long exhale feels calming.

You feel it within a minute or two.

Well established

Moves lymph and blood

Each time the diaphragm drops, pressure in the chest falls and pressure in the belly rises. This pushes lymph up the thoracic duct (more on this below) and also helps blood in the big veins flow back to the heart.

Happens with every deep breath.

Well established

Eases breathlessness

Breathing out slowly through pursed lips keeps the airways open for longer and empties the lungs better. NHS lung-rehab classes teach it to people with COPD and asthma.

Helps straight away when you're puffed.

Good trials

Lowers stress and lifts mood

Trials of short daily breathing practice show people feel less anxious and in a better mood. In a 2023 Stanford study, 5 minutes a day of slow, exhale-focused breathing improved mood over a month.

Builds over 2 to 4 weeks of daily practice.

Good trials

Can bring blood pressure down

Slow breathing lowers blood pressure while you do it. With regular daily practice over several weeks, studies show a small fall of a few points. It adds to treatment; it doesn't replace it.

Builds over weeks. Keep taking any medication.

Early evidence

Helps with reflux and digestion

Part of the diaphragm wraps around the bottom of the food pipe and helps keep stomach acid down. Small trials found belly-breathing practice eased reflux symptoms. The calmer "rest and digest" state also helps the gut.

Small studies so far, but promising.

Early evidence

Better sleep

Slow breathing in bed slows the heart and quietens a busy mind, which helps some people fall asleep sooner. A short session before bed is a gentle habit to try.

Small studies; worth trying for yourself.

Early evidence

Supports your core and back

The diaphragm is the roof of your core muscles, with the pelvic floor as the floor. Breathing well with it helps them work together, and it is used in some back-pain and pelvic-floor rehab.

Early evidence, mostly as part of physio.

Early evidence

Takes the edge off pain

Slow breathing can make pain feel less intense, probably through the same calming effect on the nervous system. It is used alongside other care for long-term pain.

Small studies; effects vary between people.

What it won't do

It doesn't "detox" you, cure illness or replace medicine. Fast, forceful breathwork, such as rapid breathing with long breath-holds, is a different practice and can make you faint. Never do that kind in water or while driving.

Slow, gentle and comfortable is the whole idea here.

How to do it

  1. Sit tall or lie on your back with knees bent. Loosen anything tight at the waist.
  2. Optional warm-up: with a flat, light hand, stroke gently down into the hollow just above each collarbone, 10 slow times a side. Skin-deep pressure only.
  3. Put one hand on your chest and one on your belly, just below the ribs.
  4. Breathe in through your nose. Let the belly hand rise. The chest hand barely moves.
  5. Hold softly at the top if the pattern asks. Never strain or clench.
  6. Breathe out slowly through pursed lips, as if through a straw. Let the belly fall, and at the very end draw it gently in.
  7. Start with 3 to 5 minutes. Once or twice a day is a common routine.

Why it may help lymph flow

Lymph has no heart to pump it. It moves when muscles squeeze the vessels, when the vessels contract on their own, and when pressure in the body changes.

When the diaphragm drops on a deep in-breath, pressure in the chest falls and pressure in the belly rises. That squeezes lymph from the cisterna chyli in the abdomen up the thoracic duct, which empties into a vein at the base of the neck on the left side.

That is why deep breathing is part of most lymphoedema self-care routines. The physiology is well established; studies on long-term results are small, so treat this as one helpful habit alongside moving, walking and any care plan you already have.

Stop, or check first

  • If you feel dizzy, light-headed or tingly, stop and breathe normally. Shorter steps help.
  • If you have lymphoedema, have had lymph nodes removed or treated, are pregnant, or have a heart, lung or blood pressure condition, ask your GP or lymphoedema nurse before adding holds.
  • New or sudden swelling, especially in one leg with pain, warmth or redness, needs a doctor the same day. Breathing exercises are not a treatment for it.
